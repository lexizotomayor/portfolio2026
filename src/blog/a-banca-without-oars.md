For years, living on an island meant sharing a single frame of time with people I rarely called close friends, yet knew by heart. They were the faces across the table during board meetings at the port, or in the offices along Middle Road, Capital Hill, and Beach Road. On an island small enough that work, errands, and rest all occupy the same few miles, our paths crossed constantly. I ran into them at lunch in local restaurants, passed them along the shaded walkways of American Memorial Park, or saw them in hotel dining rooms during community gatherings—the very places I went to work, eat, and catch my breath.

We did not need to be intimates to be connected. Moving through the same landmarks—breathing the same salt air, weathering the same seasons, living through the same unremarkable Tuesdays—spun an invisible thread between us. Each person held a corner of our collective memory, anchoring that era so it stayed whole.

When word comes that one of them has died, the grief is hard to name. It differs from the collapse of losing family; it is the sudden snapping of a thread. Maurice Halbwachs wrote that memory relies on shared social frameworks—other people who remember the same time and place with us. Without them, a piece of that era loses its witness.

It feels, too, like a game we played as children: joining hands in a tight circle and spinning together, faster and faster, held upright by the pull of everyone else's weight. For a while, the momentum feels endless. Then one grip slips, and someone breaks away. Another lets go. Martin Heidegger observed how much our sense of being at home in the world depends on simply being-with others in a shared space. When enough grips slip, the circle opens, the spinning stops, and those of us still standing are left breathless and dizzy—like a *banca* adrift without its oars, still floating on the same water, yet missing what once kept it steady and pointed toward shore.
