*Night Train to Lisbon* pulls you back by the elbow. Pascal Mercier writes:

> “We leave something of ourselves behind when we leave a place, we stay there, even though we go away. And there are things in us that we can find again only by going back there.”

Carl Jung would have argued the opposite. For him, meaning was never inside the building itself — we simply pour our own inner lives into brick and pavement until the address feels charged with our memories.

I don't think either of them has the whole answer. Something does stay snagged in a place, the way Mercier says. But going back is never a simple retrieval. It's closer to holding the story you've carried in your head up against the actual walls and floors, and seeing if it still fits.

Sometimes the place goes first. A corner store left to decay, or a super typhoon taking it whole, leaving nowhere left to check your memories against.

In the book, Amadeu de Prado calls a former home a time capsule. Walk into an apartment you lived in ten years ago, and the wood floor still bears the scuffs of your boots — a mark you left behind long after you turned over the keys. That old habit stays rooted there. Standing in that doorway, comparing how you remembered things to what is actually in front of you, is where the past meets the present, and the memory either holds or it breaks.

My own version of that threshold wasn't a home. It was the office on Alaihai Avenue, and unlike Prado's apartment, nothing there has fallen apart. The building still stands. The company still operates out of it. A few people I used to sit near are probably still at their desks. Nothing forced my memory of the place to break. I simply stopped being the version of myself who belonged there — and the room doesn't know that. It's still holding space for a person who no longer exists, while somebody else sits in the chair.
