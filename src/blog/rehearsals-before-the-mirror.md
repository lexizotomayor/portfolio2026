There she was on local television, reading the news in a tongue I did not yet know. Her accent belonged neither to Manila nor to America, yet it held me fast—firm, clear, and direct. On weekends, I sat behind a small wooden desk, faced the mirror, and began my own quiet broadcast, reading paragraphs aloud to play the part of the woman on the screen. That routine gave my early English a firm footing.

In grade school we had *Nation Builder*, a slender paper filled with simplified accounts of current events. Reading those short dispatches gave me something to say to the room. Before long, I moved on to Manila’s broadsheets and a clearer view of the world outside. My family had modest means, but my parents made sure two major dailies landed on our step every morning. Reading them stretched my thinking and gave shape to my sentences.

In high school I wanted a spot on the campus paper. I was hardly the finest writer in the room, yet my name ended up in the masthead. By my junior year, Miss Violet Lauron, our adviser, was taking me along to the printer in Quezon City—a place called Bookman—where we sat over galley proofs long into the night before the press ran.

Managing editorial work suited me. I took to it early, so stepping into the role professionally years later felt no stranger than slipping on an old coat. I knew the rhythm of the work in my bones.

That certainty—or perhaps an overreach of youth—faced its true trial later in Saipan, where every newsroom crisis brought back those early years at the copy desk.

Looking back, I held the reporter in me at arm’s length during college. Reading the student paper, I felt a familiar mix of wonder and envy, but I kept to my history and political essays. Writing exam papers on the causes of Asian conflicts was hard labor, and I was often surprised by what came off the tip of my pen. Above all, I labored over the names: Durand Line, Khyber Pass, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, Qin Shi Huang Di, Nebuchadnezzar, Deng Xiaoping, Oda Nobunaga, Empress Dowager Cixi, Zhuang Zi, Kong Zi, Mimamsa, Vedanta, Pitakka.

One step led to the next. I never became Tina Monzon-Palma, but the dream fulfilled itself in its own way. Those mornings with the broadsheets, and those long hours broadcasting to the  mirror, were not idle play. They were simply the first trial runs for a life spent with the news.
