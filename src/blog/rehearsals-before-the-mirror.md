There she was on local television, reading the news in a tongue I did not yet know. Her accent belonged neither to Manila nor to America, yet it held me fast—at once firm, clear, and direct. On weekends, I sat behind a small wooden desk, faced the mirror, and began my own quiet rehearsal, reading paragraphs aloud to play the part of the woman on the screen. The habit gave my early English its first real footing.

In grade school we had *Nation Builder*, a slender paper filled with simplified accounts of current events. Reading those short dispatches gave me something to say to the room. Before long, I moved on to Manila’s broadsheets and a clearer view of the world outside. We were a family of modest means, but my parents saw to it that two major dailies landed on our step every morning. Reading them stretched my mind and gave shape to my sentences.

In high school I wanted a spot on the campus paper. I was hardly the finest writer in the room, yet my name landed in the masthead. By my junior year, Miss Violet Lauron, our adviser, was taking me along to the printer in Quezon City—a place called Bookman—where we sat over galley proofs long into the evening before the press ran.

Managing editorial work suited me. I took to it so early that stepping into the role professionally years later felt no stranger than slipping on an old coat. I knew the rhythm of the work in my bones.

That certainty—or perhaps an overreach of youth—faced its true trial later in Saipan, where every newsroom crisis called up those early years at the copy desk.

Looking back, I held the reporter in me at arm’s length during college. Reading the student paper, I felt a familiar mix of wonder and envy, but I kept to my history and political essays. Writing exam papers on the causes of Asian conflicts was hard labor, and I was often surprised by what came off the tip of my pen. Above all, I labored over the names: Durand Line, Khyber Pass, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, Qin Shi Huang Di, Nebuchadnezzar, Deng Xiaoping, Oda Nobunaga, Empress Dowager Cixi, Zhuang Zi, Kong Zi, Mimamsa, Vedanta, Pitakka.

One step led to the next. I never became Tina Monzon-Palma, but the dream fulfilled itself in its own way. Those mornings with the broadsheets, and those long afternoons practicing behind the glass, were not idle play. They were simply the first rehearsal for a life spent with the news.
