In the photograph, two nuns in dark habits, one in a white veil and the other in black, hurry down a dirt road in Baguio alongside a helmeted soldier, their arms weighed down with heavy sacks and bundles. Behind them, tall pine trees stand stripped and splintered beside the ruined shell of a building. Looking at that creased sepia print on my desk, I was back in 1997.

Back then, my friends Joey and Nora and I were in the thick of putting together experimental high school textbooks in math and science translated into Filipino. Tired from long days at our desks in Quezon City and hungry for air that did not smell like exhaust and damp paper, we acted like the Three Stooges on a whim, threw a few things into overnight bags,and caught a bus north.

It was my first time in Baguio, the cool mountain city that had served as the American summer capital before the war.From the bus station, we took a cab straight to Teachers Camp, a sprawling compound of old wooden cottages and dormitories tucked into the pines. We had three days to do nothing at all.

The long, winding climb up Kennon Road had wrung us out. The moment we reached our room, all three of us dropped onto our beds and fell asleep.

I do not know how long I was out before a gentle knock woke me. Still groggy, I dragged myself out from under the blankets, walked across the wooden floor, and turned the knob. When the door swung open, two nuns stood quietly on the threshold. They spoke to me, though whatever they said vanished the second I heard it.

Behind me, I heard the low murmur of Joey and Nora talking. I blinked, and the doorway was empty. I had been dreaming, though the cold mountain air felt real against my skin. When I told Joey and Nora about the two sisters at the door, the room went quiet, and goosebumps broke out along my arms.

I have never been one to go looking for ghost stories. Still, those two quiet figures stayed with me long after we rode the bus back down to the heat of the city down in the plains.

As I stared at the photograph of nuns clutching their earthly belongings as they clamber uphill to safety, the memory of Teachers' Camp returned like a cold draft, how on a chilly afternoon in 1997, I had a warm welcome from two sisters who dared cross the great divide.
