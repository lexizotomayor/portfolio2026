If you walk through Garapan on a sunny afternoon, it is hard to miss the grand superstructure sitting in the center of town. Built during a season of high ambition, the unfinished palace was designed to solve the island’s economic future in a single, dramatic move. Today, it stands as a reminder of how quickly single-industry ventures can change, leaving behind millions of square feet of concrete, hotel towers, and unoccupied worker apartments across our villages.

Looking at this vast footprint, we do not have to wait for another mega-project to revive it. Instead of treating these properties as a problem tied up in courts, we can view them as a clean slate for a broader economic mosaic. A mosaic is built from many small, durable pieces laid side by side. When one piece shifts, the rest of the picture holds. By taking a practical approach to repurposing this real estate, we can turn empty square footage into useful, community-centered assets.

The first step is separating the gaming license from the physical land and buildings. Once the structure is viewed simply as prime Pacific real estate rather than an exclusive casino, local leaders, court receivers, and planners gain the freedom to divide the space into manageable, multi-use parcels. The ground floor—once intended for gaming tables—has the scale to become a regional convention hall, federal training center, and cultural exposition space. Partitioning the facility allows local entrepreneurs, trade shows, regional conferences, and government agencies to share modern infrastructure without carrying the cost alone.

Upper hotel towers can then be opened in phases by mid-sized, boutique hospitality groups. Blending small-scale hotels with public art galleries, heritage displays, and local dining gives visitors an authentic taste of Chamorro and Carolinian culture while creating steady jobs. At the same time, the off-site residential properties scattered through the villages offer immediate help for the community. With straightforward legal resolution, these dormant apartment complexes and dormitories can be converted into long-term rental housing, providing clean, accessible homes for teachers, nurses, utility technicians, and young families.

When we replace a single mega-monolith with dozens of interconnected uses—a convention center, a local coffee shop on the ground floor, vocational training rooms, and affordable housing for healthcare workers—we build an island economy that stands secure through every season. The grand structure in Garapan does not need to remain an idle memory. With a proactive spirit and a commitment to human scale, we can return those empty square feet to the daily rhythm of island life, creating an economic mosaic built to last.
